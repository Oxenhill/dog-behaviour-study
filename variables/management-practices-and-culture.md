## Crate/Confinement Practices

### Germany -- Tierschutz-Hundeverordnung (Animal Welfare Dog Ordinance)
| Date | Requirement | Source |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 1 Jan 2022 (current version in force; an earlier version of the ordinance predates this -- exact original enactment year NOT independently confirmed this session, flagged as a gap) | Max 2 hours/day permitted in a box/crate | VDH (Verband fur das Deutsche Hundewesen -- the German Kennel Club's animal welfare page) + Ramstein Air Base official fact sheet (US military, describing German law to personnel) -- [quality: A/B] |
| Same | Minimum daily contact: at least 2x/day, 2 hours total, of owner contact (play/walk/dog school) | Ramstein AF fact sheet -- [quality: B, describing primary law] |
| Same | Kennelled dogs must additionally get 2x1 hour/day free-running time | Ramstein AF fact sheet -- [quality: B] |
| Same | Minimum floor space by withers height: up to 50cm = 6m2; 50-65cm = 8m2; over 65cm = 10m2 | Ramstein AF fact sheet -- [quality: B] |
| Proposed 2020, drafts by early 2021 | Prohibits extended tethering (chains/leashes) except for working dogs | ALDF (Animal Legal Defense Fund) -- [quality: B, advocacy org describing draft legislation at the time] |
| Same | Outdoor-kept dogs require thermally insulated shelter | Ramstein AF fact sheet -- [quality: B] |
| Ongoing | Quiet-hours barking limits: 10pm-6am and 1-3pm, max 10 consecutive/30 cumulative minutes of noise outside those hours | Ramstein AF fact sheet -- [quality: B] |
| Primary legal text (not extracted this session) | Full statute at gesetze-im-internet.de/tierschhuv/ | Identified, official German government legislation database -- [quality: A, NOT yet extracted -- next-session target for exact original enactment date and full text verification] |

**Significance:** this is the single most concrete, quantified husbandry-regulation regime found in the whole study -- Germany doesn't just restrict crate TIME, it mandates minimum daily human-contact hours AND minimum floor space AND bans extended tethering, all in one instrument. This is a genuinely different regulatory philosophy from the US/UK/Australia approach (which regulate almost none of this at a national level) and is a strong candidate explanatory variable.

### Sweden -- Djurskyddslagen (Animal Welfare Act), via Lansstyrelsen (County Administrative Board) guidance
| Rule | Detail | Source |
|------|--------|--------|
| Home confinement | **Fully banned, zero exceptions, zero duration** -- "You may never shut a dog or cat in a cage at home. Not even for a short while." A crate may only be present as a resting place if the door is removed or permanently fixed open | Lansstyrelsen official PDF, "Hund och katt i bur -- sa sager djurskyddsreglerna" -- [quality: A, primary Swedish government source] |
| Transport | Permitted; crate time capped at 3 hours while the vehicle is stationary | Same source -- [quality: A] |
| Hunting | Permitted up to 8 hours/day, with a break at least every 3 hours | Same source -- [quality: A] |
| Events/shows/competition training | Permitted; crate must be covered on at least 2 sides; dog must be exercised at least every 2 hours | Same source -- [quality: A] |

**Correction to the popular framing:** most secondary/SEO sources (dog forums, "why is X banned" listicles) state simply "crates are illegal in Sweden," which is imprecise. The primary government source shows a purpose-specific, time-limited framework -- home confinement is an absolute zero-tolerance ban, but transport/hunting/event use is explicitly permitted within stated time limits. This distinction matters for the study: it is not "Sweden bans crates," it is "Sweden bans using a crate as a routine home-management tool but permits it as a short-duration functional tool" -- a genuinely different, more precise variable than the country-file shorthand would suggest.

### Finland
Repeatedly cited alongside Sweden as also restricting/banning routine home crating (multiple secondary sources, e.g. a Swedish reader's testimony quoted in Patricia McConnell's piece: "Dogs are not allowed to be locked into crates unless it's during training or traveling"). **No primary Finnish government source (equivalent to the Swedish Lansstyrelsen PDF) was located this session** -- flagged as a gap, not asserted as confirmed fact at the same confidence level as the Swedish entry.

### USA / UK / Australia / Netherlands
No national or state/province-level restriction on home crate use was located in any of these countries -- crate training is a widespread, culturally normalised and often actively recommended practice (e.g. via APDT-affiliated trainers in the US and UK -- see variables/dog-trainer-regulation.md). This is a genuine, confirmed structural contrast with Germany/Sweden/Finland, not a data gap -- absence of a restriction was checked for, not merely unsearched.

## Leash Law Strictness & Off-Leash Culture

### United States
| Finding | Detail | Source |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Popular perception vs. legal reality | Only **2 states** (Michigan, Pennsylvania) have a statewide leash mandate. Most states instead prohibit dogs "running at large" (unsupervised/uncontrolled), which is a materially different and looser standard -- voice/hand-command control can legally substitute for a physical leash in many jurisdictions | Animal Legal & Historical Center, "Table of State Dog Leash Laws" -- [quality: A/B, academic legal-reference database] |
| Local variation | Leash requirements are overwhelmingly a MUNICIPAL/county matter, not state or federal -- creating a highly inconsistent patchwork rather than a uniform national culture | Same source |
| Public-space access (distinct from leash law) | Despite looser on-paper leash law, US CULTURAL norms around dogs in shared public space (restaurants, transit, retail) are markedly more restrictive than European norms -- this is a norms/culture gap, not a codified-law gap | Patricia McConnell (certified applied animal behaviorist), cross-country observational piece -- [quality: B, expert professional observation, not a legal survey] |

**This directly complicates the user's own working example.** The US is NOT uniformly "leash-law strict" in the way it is commonly perceived to be -- what's actually distinctive about the US is restrictive PUBLIC-SPACE-ACCESS culture (dogs excluded from restaurants/shops/transit) combined with heavy reliance on crate-based home management, rather than a strict national leash regime. This is exactly the kind of nuance the data-integrity rule exists to surface rather than smooth over into the simpler popular narrative.

### Netherlands
| Finding | Detail | Source |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Default | On-leash required throughout built-up areas: streets, squares, children's playgrounds, sports fields, bathing beaches (summer), nature reserves during bird-breeding season (roughly March-July) | nlcompass.com expat guide -- [quality: B/C, expat-guide secondary source, not a primary legal text] |
| Off-leash provision | Every Dutch municipality designates specific off-leash zones (losloopgebieden) -- dog fields, some parks, nature areas, clearly signposted; owners must still keep the dog "under control" and clean up | Same source |
| Beach seasonality | Year-round dedicated dog beaches (hondenstranden) exist; on general beaches, off-leash allowed mid-Oct to May, restricted/leash-only in summer (May-Sept) | Same source |
| Cultural enforcement | Strict social (not just legal) enforcement of poop-cleanup norms -- described as drawing visible public disapproval if not followed | Same source |

**Structural contrast with the US:** the Netherlands runs a formalised, municipality-by-municipality DESIGNATED off-leash zone system (a specific place is either on-leash-by-default or off-leash-by-designation, unambiguously signposted) -- versus the US's much vaguer "running at large" standard with voice-control exceptions. Both nominally restrict off-leash freedom, but via structurally different mechanisms -- this is a genuinely different variable than a simple "leash law: yes/no" flag would capture.

### United Kingdom
Patricia McConnell's observational account (not a legal survey) describes off-leash dogs as common in London parks, and dogs permitted in pubs and on buses -- practices she notes "would never be allowed in the US." **This is one expert's cross-country travel observation, not a systematic legal comparison** -- flagged at appropriately lower confidence than the Swedish/German government-sourced entries above, and a target for a primary-source (UK local authority byelaw survey) follow-up.

### Sweden
Per the same McConnell piece: dogs allowed off-leash in many areas but must be "under control" -- consistent with (though not as precisely sourced as) the Netherlands' designated-zone model. Not independently verified against a primary Swedish source this session.

### Australia
Council-by-council designated off-leash areas confirmed to exist (e.g. Melbourne-area councils, Townsville's "Off-leash areas for dogs" subordinate local law) -- structurally similar to the Dutch losloopgebieden model (formal designation by local government) rather than the US's vaguer standard.

**Session 6 update -- concrete detailed example EXTRACTED (Randwick City Council, NSW):**
| Finding | Detail | Source |
|---------|--------|--------|

| Default rule | "Dogs must be kept on a leash at all times when they are not in a designated leash free area" and must remain "under effective control" | Same source |

This confirms Australia belongs firmly in the same "on-leash-default + designated, signed, mapped exception zones" typology bucket as the Netherlands and Canada (Calgary) -- the Randwick model is close to a direct structural match for Calgary's, down to the interactive map and brochure format.

## Interpretive Note for Phase 5 (Analysis)
This file surfaces a candidate explanatory axis that cuts across the density/ownership-rate/BSL variables already built: **management philosophy**. Two dimensions have emerged with enough cross-country evidence (Session 3+4) to name explicitly:

**Dimension 1 -- home confinement culture:** "confine-and-control at home, restrict access to shared public space" (USA pattern: crate-normalised, patchy/loose leash law, but restrictive shared-space access) vs. "restrict home confinement, formalise but broaden public off-leash access" (Sweden/Germany/Netherlands pattern: crate time-limited or banned at home, but designated off-leash zones and more shared-space inclusion). Germany adds a third, distinct element neither the US nor Sweden has: a quantified MINIMUM daily exercise/contact mandate.

**Dimension 2 -- public-leash regulatory model (Session 4 addition, revised Session 6), now with FOUR distinct types evidenced and CONFIRMED (not "arguably") country assignments:**
1. **Leash-always, no off-leash exception** -- Japan. The most restrictive model in the dataset.
2. **On-leash-by-default, off-leash-by-designated-zone** -- Netherlands, Canada (Calgary), Australia (Randwick, confirmed Session 6), and New Zealand (confirmed Session 6, council-delegated). Four countries now firmly evidenced in this bucket -- a formalised, signposted, municipally-administered exception system appears to be the MODAL pattern across the English-speaking/Commonwealth + Netherlands countries in this dataset.
3. **Off-leash-by-default (or control-based, not leash-based), leash-by-exception** -- Norway (seasonal only), and France's "divagation"/100m-voice-control standard, which is control-based rather than leash-based even outside the seasonal rural rule. The loose end of the US "running at large" standard sits closer to this model than to model 2.
4. **Hybrid/two-tier or hybrid/multi-mechanism** -- France and Finland layer a PERMANENT urban/dangerous-dog leash rule on top of a SEASONAL rural wildlife-protection rule. **Denmark (revised Session 6) also belongs in this bucket**, via a different mechanism: a permanent on-leash default in ordinary public spaces, PLUS a seasonal beach exception (winter off-leash/summer on-leash) PLUS a large network of 200+ permanently-designated off-leash forests. This makes hybrid/multi-mechanism models the pattern found in at least 4 of the 6 Northern/Western European countries reviewed (France, Norway, Finland, Denmark) -- only Japan (leash-always) and, partially, Sweden/UK (thin sourcing, not yet resolved either way) sit clearly outside it.

Whether any of these management-philosophy or leash-model clusters correlates with the bite/surrender/referral metrics already collected is a Phase 5 question -- this file only establishes the variables themselves, dated and sourced; it does not draw the correlation. **The Session 4 "Denmark near-maximal strictness" pairing note is RETRACTED as of Session 6** -- with the seasonal beach exception and dog-forest network now documented, Denmark's leash regime is no longer an outlier at the strict end; it converges with Norway/Finland/France's hybrid pattern instead. This means Denmark's PLOS ONE breed-legislation null result can no longer be tentatively explained away by "leash law is already doing the work instead" -- Denmark's leash regime is now evidenced as comparably permissive (in a zoned/seasonal sense) to its Nordic neighbours, several of which have NOT been tested for a comparable BSL effect. This is a genuine open question for Phase 5, not a resolved one: **why did Denmark's 2010 BSL show no effect, given a management-culture profile that is not obviously more or less restrictive than comparator countries?** Candidate directions for Phase 5 to pursue: enforcement-intensity differences (not yet measured anywhere in this dataset -- see Data Gaps), breed-population-composition changes independent of the ban, or the specific 13-breed list's poor overlap with the dogs actually implicated in bites (a mechanism suggested, but not confirmed, by the France finding above that breed was NOT a significant severity risk factor in that country's own bite-severity survey).

**A second cross-file link worth flagging for Phase 5:** Canada's Manitoba BSL study (data/by-country/Canada.md, extracted Session 6) found a suggestive-but-not-statistically-significant-on-simple-pre/post-test population-level association between BSL and reduced hospitalisations, strongest in children under 20. This sits between Denmark's clean null result and a hypothetical clear positive result -- with only two BSL-effectiveness studies in the dataset so far, both nuanced rather than clean-cut, the honest Phase 5 framing is "the peer-reviewed evidence on BSL effectiveness is genuinely mixed/weak across the two jurisdictions studied," not a one-line verdict either way.
